1 Background: cohesion and behavior in school classrooms

Van den Bos, Crone, Meuwese, and Güroğlu (2018) asked how adolescents’ network positions and classroom cohesion relate to peer-reported social behavior and generalized trust. They recruited 611 students from 22 classes in one Dutch secondary school, with a mean age of 14.8 years. They report excluding two mixed classes whose membership changed over time, leaving 496 participants (article, pp. 3 and 10).

Students nominated classmates they liked most, without a fixed nomination limit and without self-nominations. Other questions measured helping, cooperation, aggression, victimization, and perceived popularity. An economic game measured trust in unfamiliar others. The authors used mutual liking: an undirected edge existed only when both students nominated one another (pp. 3 and 10–11; S2 Text, item 1).

The authors measured cohesion with mutual-network density, diameter, and clusters. Denser mutual networks were associated with less antisocial behavior; larger classes with more antisocial behavior. Fewer clusters and shorter diameter were associated with greater generalized trust (pp. 5–6). At the individual level, eigenvector centrality was associated with more prosocial behavior and relational aggression and less victimization; no individual centrality measure was associated with trust (pp. 7–8; Table 3 and S4). The cross-sectional design does not identify causation (p. 9).

[Despite the title’s wording, the authors describe the direct classroom-cohesion association with prosocial behavior as a trend in the discussion (p. 9). I do not present that as an established causal effect.]

My application keeps the directions. I use all sixteen released students in archive group 36 to distinguish sending nominations, receiving nominations, and residual reciprocity. I model neither helping nor trust, and I am not replicating the paper’s cross-classroom behavioral regressions.

[Question: How are liking nominations associated with matching recorded sex and age differences, and what changes when I add multiplicative structure beyond actor tendencies and residual reciprocity? By the end, I want to be able to construct aligned network inputs, explain the two model calls, and interpret results together with their diagnostics.]

3 Describe and visualize the network

3.1 Size, reciprocity, and transitivity

[An actor is a student. A dyad is a pair of students; this directed matrix has 240 ordered non-self entries within 120 unordered student pairs. Y[i, j] records whether student i nominates student j. Out-degree counts nominations sent; in-degree counts nominations received. Directed density divides ties by n(n - 1), while mutual undirected density divides edges by n(n - 1)/2. Reciprocity is the fraction of directed nominations with a reverse nomination. Directed transitivity here is the fraction of distinct-student two-paths i -> j -> k with i -> k. I state the denominator rather than treating all transitivity definitions as equivalent (igraph documentation).]

n_students <- igraph::vcount(g)
n_ties <- igraph::ecount(g)
out_degree <- igraph::degree(g, mode = "out", loops = FALSE)
in_degree <- igraph::degree(g, mode = "in", loops = FALSE)
reciprocated_fraction <- igraph::reciprocity(g, ignore.loops = TRUE, mode = "default")

# [A %*% A counts directed two-paths. Removing its diagonal keeps i different from k.]
two_paths <- A %*% A
diag(two_paths) <- 0
n_two_paths <- sum(two_paths)
n_closed_paths <- sum(two_paths * A)
directed_transitivity <- n_closed_paths / n_two_paths

# [This separate undirected graph follows the paper's mutual-tie rule.]
M <- A * t(A)
g_mutual <- igraph::graph_from_adjacency_matrix(M, mode = "undirected", weighted = NULL, diag = FALSE)
mutual_pairs <- igraph::ecount(g_mutual)
mutual_density <- igraph::edge_density(g_mutual, loops = FALSE)
mutual_transitivity <- igraph::transitivity(g_mutual, type = "global")
descriptives <- data.frame(

For a binary probit model, Y[i,j] = 1 when the latent propensity is positive:

zij = β0 + βssameij + βa|agei − agej| + ai + bj + uiTvj + εij.

Component Meaning in this classroom Code/result
Measured coefficients Conditional association with matching sex code and age difference Xdyad, BETA
Sender effect Residual tendency to nominate classmates rvar, APM, variance va
Receiver effect Residual tendency to receive nominations cvar, BPM, variance vb
Sender–receiver covariance Association between the same student’s two tendencies cab
Residual reciprocity Correlation between opposite-direction latent errors within a pair dcor, rho
Multiplicative contribution Higher-order patterns beyond the predictors and additive/dyadic terms R, U, V, UVPM

INA §10.5 calls AME a latent-factor model. Week 10 introduced Euclidean distance (slide 4) and a bilinear inner-product representation (slides 20–21). These calls use separate sender/receiver vectors, not a Euclidean-distance term. Additive and dyadic components represent actor heterogeneity and reciprocity; multiplicative effects can represent higher-order patterns (Dorff et al., 2020, pp. 484–485; Minhas et al., 2019; Hoff, 2021).

[The readings may place a diagonal scaling matrix between factors; the installed directed implementation computes U %*% t(V). That scaling can be absorbed algebraically into unrestricted vectors without establishing identical priors. R = 0 removes only the multiplicative part. R = 2 can represent shared-partner and triadic patterns but does not guarantee transitivity or identify a trait. R is the multiplicative dimension, not a distance dimension or mixture-group count. The residual variance is fixed at one for probit identification. Given model parameters and latent effects, different unordered residual pairs are independent, but opposite directions within a pair may correlate through rho. prior = list() retains the package’s default, data-calibrated priors.]

4.2 Step 2: Fit two rank-zero chains live

[These calls run both when I execute the chunk and when I knit the lesson. In the October 29 run they took 108.63 and 105.84 seconds, about 3.57 minutes combined; the table prints the times for the current run. Other computers may differ. A chain is a sequence of simulated parameter values. I discard the first 10,000 iterations (burn), run 50,000 more (nscan), and retain every 25th (odens): 2,000 saved draws per chain. Saving less often does not make those draws independent. I keep the checked settings (amen::ame, version 1.4.5).]

4.4 Step 4: Check sampling and summarize the estimates

# [Each column of x is a chain; I do not concatenate chains before calculating R-hat.]
summarize_model <- function(fits, model) {
  parameters <- c(colnames(fits[[1]]$BETA), "va", "vb", "cab", "rho")
  rows <- lapply(parameters, function(p) {
    x <- sapply(fits, function(f) {
      if (p %in% colnames(f$BETA)) f$BETA[, p] else f$VC[, p]
    })
    data.frame(model = model, parameter = p, mean = mean(x),
      lower = unname(quantile(x, .025)), upper = unname(quantile(x, .975)),
      rhat = posterior::rhat(x), bulk_ess = posterior::ess_bulk(x),
      tail_ess = posterior::ess_tail(x), mcse_over_sd = posterior::mcse_mean(x) / sd(c(x)))
  })
  do.call(rbind, rows)
}

[The central 95% predictive interval contains the middle 95% of a statistic across the saved replicated networks. It concerns replicated data, whereas a credible interval concerns a parameter. The installed implementation returns five GOF statistics even though the brief help description mentions four.]

predictive_table <- function(fits, label) {
  observed <- fits[[1]]$GOF[1, ]
  stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(observed, fits[[2]]$GOF[1, ])))
  # [Row 1 is observed; I exclude it from all replicated-draw calculations.]
  draws <- do.call(rbind, lapply(fits, function(f) f$GOF[-1, , drop = FALSE]))
  intervals <- apply(draws, 2, quantile, probs = c(.025, .975))
  share <- colMeans(sweep(draws, 2, observed, ">="))
  data.frame(model = label, statistic = names(observed), observed = unname(observed),
    replicated_mean = colMeans(draws), lower = intervals[1, ], upper = intervals[2, ],
    rep_ge_obs = unname(share), row.names = NULL)
}

5 Connect the results to classroom cohesion

The paper represents cohesion using mutual-network density, diameter, and clusters (p. 3). My directed graph retains one-way reports as well: 56 of its 96 nominations belong to 28 mutual pairs. Applying the paper’s mutual rule gives density 0.233 rather than 0.400, and global transitivity 0.552.

[Student 1040 sends 9 nominations and receives 0. That student remains in both graphs but is an isolate only in the mutual graph. The mutual construction removes the nine one-way nominations; the directed model retains them as observations. They are not attributable to the sender effect alone.]

[Developmental connection: Directed and mutual measures reveal different aspects of adolescents’ relational opportunities. These liking reports do not establish whether student 1040 lacks support, resources, or relationships outside this classroom. Reciprocated and closed patterns are consistent with local cohesion, but do not establish uniform cohesion or a causal effect on cooperation. This application models neither prosocial behavior nor trust.]
